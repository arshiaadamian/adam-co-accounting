Ask most small business owners how their numbers look and you'll get one of two answers: "Great!" or "I have no idea." The difference between those two answers is usually bookkeeping.

## What Bookkeeping Actually Is

Bookkeeping is the process of recording every financial transaction in your business — money in, money out, what you're owed, and what you owe. It's not glamorous, but it's the foundation that everything else — taxes, financial statements, planning — is built on.

## Why It Matters Beyond Tax Season

Most people think of bookkeeping as something you deal with once a year at tax time. In reality, up-to-date books give you a live view of your business:

- **Cash flow:** Know when you're at risk of running short before it becomes a crisis.
- **Profitability:** Understand which products, clients, or services actually make you money.
- **Better decisions:** Whether to hire, invest, or cut costs — good decisions require good data.
- **Financing:** Lenders and investors require accurate financials. Scrambling to reconstruct a year of records is expensive and error-prone.

## The Cost of Letting It Slide

When books fall behind, catching up is painful. You or your accountant has to reconstruct transactions from memory, bank statements, and stacks of receipts. This costs time and money, and often results in missed deductions or errors.

More importantly, you lose visibility into your business for however long the books were behind. You can't make good decisions with stale data.

## How Often Should You Do It?

For most small businesses, a monthly bookkeeping routine is the sweet spot:

1. Reconcile bank and credit card accounts
2. Categorize transactions
3. Review a quick profit-and-loss statement

If your business is high-volume, weekly may make sense. If it's very simple, quarterly might work — but anything less frequent and you're flying blind.
